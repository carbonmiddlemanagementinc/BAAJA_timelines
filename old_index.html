How federal reviews stack up, before and after BAAJA

Six representative projects run through current law and through the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026: a gas pipeline, a geothermal plant, onshore and offshore wind farms, a solar project and an interstate transmission line. Switch between current law and the bill on one chart, and hover or tap any bar for the rule or data behind it.

Illustrative. Durations combine statutory deadlines, published averages, case histories and stated assumptions, which are flagged in each step's details. "Deadlines met" assumes agencies meet each regime's deadlines. "Delays and challenges" draws on recent cases, including federal actions in 2025 and 2026. Interconnection, power contracts, tax credits and resource risk are not modeled, and state permits appear only where they set the pace. Interconnection alone took the median plant built in 2025 about 61 months from request to operation (LBNL).

How to read it

  • One chart per project. "Show" picks the regime drawn in full; the same step under the other regime appears as a thin outline in the same row, so a bar that ends further left finishes sooner. "Both" draws the two regimes as two lanes. Steps that exist under only one regime are tagged. The chip after a step's name gives the change in when that step ends under BAAJA.
  • Solid bars are scheduled work. Light bars with an outline are open windows, such as the time a lawsuit can still be filed. Striped bars are delays that could happen but are not counted.
  • In "Deadlines met," the main environmental review runs about the same length under both regimes, so the comparison isolates the bill's other changes. Where the bill starts the 2-year clock earlier, the review ends a little earlier.
  • In "Delays and challenges," the wind, solar, transmission and offshore reviews run as long as recent cases did under current law. Under the bill they run past the deadline until the sponsor's suit forces a decision. The pipeline and geothermal reviews are held equal under both regimes.
  • Developer work before an application is complete is held equal. The bill's deadlines cut agency time, not survey or design time.
  • Diamonds mark the key federal decision and the date the project enters service or first delivers power.
  • The odds of a lawsuit are not modeled. Suits followed 28% of large energy and transport EISs finished from 2010 to 2018, including about two thirds of solar, half of pipelines, 38% of wind and 31% of transmission EISs, but only about 1 in 450 NEPA decisions of all kinds. Agencies won about 80% of appellate NEPA cases from 2013 to 2022.
  • Supporters such as FAI's Thomas Hochman expect the limits on court remedies to shorten reviews by reducing litigation-proofing. The Institute for Progress warns that shorter reviews may be more exposed to suits. The charts assume neither.

What current law already does

  • Since 2023, NEPA has required an environmental assessment within 1 year and an EIS within 2 years, counted from the earliest of the agency's decision on the level of review, its notice that a right-of-way application is complete, or the notice of intent. Agencies can extend these deadlines after consulting the applicant, and sponsors can already ask a court to enforce them (42 U.S.C. 4336a). A court can set a new deadline of up to 90 days; the Institute for Progress finds that the general remedy for agency delay, an unreasonable-delay claim under the APA, almost never succeeds. Most EISs still miss the 2-year mark: BPC found 39% of EISs finished after the 2023 law met it (BPC), and agencies reported only 2 EISs and 153 assessments as missing their deadlines in the law's first two years, a count that treats an extended deadline as met (CEQ). The bill requires the sponsor's approval for any extension and gives courts 120 days to rule.
  • Since July 2025, a sponsor can pay 125% of the agency's costs to have an EIS finished within 1 year of the notice of intent, or an assessment within 180 days (42 U.S.C. 4336f). The charts do not assume it.
  • CEQ's NEPA regulations were withdrawn in 2025, and agencies now follow their own procedures. Interior adopted interim procedures in July 2025 and final ones effective February 24, 2026 (91 FR 8738).
  • In Seven County v. Eagle County (2025), the Supreme Court told courts to defer to agencies on NEPA and said a flawed EIS does not necessarily require vacating the approval.
  • Projects covered by FAST-41 already face a 2-year limit on lawsuits (42 U.S.C. 4370m-6). Coverage is narrow: 46 projects had active timetables in March 2026, and 23 of those had been extended, 14 by more than a year (Permitting Council).
  • Interior's July 2025 memo requiring Secretary-level review of wind and solar actions, and the capacity-density test in Secretarial Order 3438, were preliminarily enjoined in April 2026 only for members of the plaintiff trade groups (Crowell). Interior has appealed to the First Circuit (CRS). A court vacated the January 2025 pause on wind permits in December 2025, and the government dropped its appeal (Harvard EELP).
  • Wind and solar projects that start construction after July 4, 2026 must be in service by the end of 2027 to claim the main federal tax credits, so none of the wind or solar timelines shown would qualify (IRS Notice 2025-42).
