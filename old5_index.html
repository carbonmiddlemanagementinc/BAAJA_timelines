How much sooner under BAAJA?

Six model projects and 148 real ones, under current law and the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026.

Each model project needs a full federal environmental review: an environmental impact statement, or for the geothermal plant an environmental assessment. Every offshore wind project needs one; most new wind and solar, built on private land, does not.

How to read the charts

  • Range chart. Open circles assume agencies already meet today's deadlines; filled circles assume the delays and suits of recent cases. The line between them is the range for each model project.
  • Real projects. Every project-specific EIS in CEQ's 2010 to 2024 data for these project types, plus Crimson Solar (missing from CEQ's file), three reviews still under way and 29 BLM geothermal exploration reviews; the 64 candidates left out, and why, are in a CSV. Each dot applies the bill's limits to the recorded dates: the EIS capped at 28 months from the NOI (the 2-year deadline plus a 4-month sponsor suit), the decision at 2 months after the final EIS, BLM's recorded wait before review at 30 days to answer an application and 4 months to the NOI, and test-well permits at 2 months. We checked why every review the limits would speed up by half a month or more ran long, against Federal Register notices, decision records, FERC orders and Permitting Dashboard records. Dots leave out months the bill would excuse or not cut: the developer's own pauses, redesigns and late filings, a wildfire, and months when no agency could act (FERC without a quorum in 2017, lapses in appropriations of more than 2 weeks), following the excuses in Sec. 1106. A thin line runs to where a dot would sit if developer changes that the records mention but do not date also counted. The replays assume developers refuse extensions they did not cause; on the Permitting Dashboard they usually agreed to them. Hollow dots are still under review and use the agency's current schedule.
  • Who this applies to. Each model project except the geothermal plant, which needs an environmental assessment, needs a federal EIS. Every offshore wind project approved so far had one, and FERC normally prepares one for a major pipeline in a new right-of-way (18 CFR 380.6). Projects with any federal NEPA review added about 10% of new solar capacity and 3.7% of new wind from 2010 to 2023 (RFF); lines needing an EIS were 3.5% of new transmission lines but 26% of new line miles from 2010 to 2020 (Niskanen). About two thirds of U.S. geothermal capacity is on BLM land (BLM).
  • Timeline. "Show" picks the regime drawn in full; the other regime appears as a thin outline in the same row. Striped bars are delays that could happen but are not counted. Diamonds mark the federal decision and the in-service date.
  • Scenarios. In "Deadlines met" agencies meet the 2-year EIS limit under both regimes, isolating the bill's other changes. In "Delays and challenges" reviews run as long as recent cases did; under the bill they run past the deadline until the sponsor's suit forces a decision. Developer work is held equal.
  • Sensitivity. Each "Delays and challenges" input rests on a few recorded cases. The sensitivity table below changes one at a time; the widest swings come from how long states take to site a transmission line, how long a FERC backstop permit takes, and how long a wind or solar EIS runs.
  • Time savings by provision. Provisions overlap, so each bar is the provision's average contribution across every order in which they could take effect (a Shapley value), and the bars add up to the total. Tooltips give the months each provision saves alone and added last.
  • Not modeled. The odds of a suit: suits followed 28% of large energy and transport EISs finished from 2010 to 2018, about two thirds of solar, half of pipelines, 38% of wind and 31% of transmission (Bennon and Wilson). Supporters such as FAI's Thomas Hochman expect the limits on court remedies to shorten reviews; the Institute for Progress warns shorter reviews may draw more suits. Interconnection, power contracts and tax credits are also not modeled.

Other views

  • Supporters stress firm deadlines and durable permits. Niskanen calls the transmission title the most consequential in two decades, citing backstop siting without DOE corridors (Niskanen); Arnold Ventures says the bill gives builders clear timelines while keeping safeguards (Arnold Ventures).
  • Critics of the historic review changes say the new Section 106 timelines are unworkable and, as drafted, "won't speed up projects" (American Cultural Resources Association); tribal preservation officers say the changes cut tribal participation (NATHPO).
  • Critics of the clean energy provisions say deadlines cannot guarantee wind and solar get built while the administration keeps blocking them (Sierra Club, SELC). The charts assume the sponsor sues and courts enforce the deadlines.
  • Environmental justice and Indigenous groups argue for funding agency staff and early community engagement instead (coalition statement). Defenders of Wildlife notes formal ESA consultation already takes about two months at the median (Defenders), consistent with the charts' finding that the ESA changes save no time.

What current law already does

  • Since 2023, NEPA has required an environmental assessment within 1 year and an EIS within 2 years, counted from the earliest of the agency's decision on the level of review, its notice that a right-of-way application is complete, or the notice of intent. Agencies can extend these deadlines after consulting the applicant, and sponsors can already ask a court to enforce them (42 U.S.C. 4336a). A court can set a new deadline of up to 90 days, but the one sponsor suit under this provision we found, over a Montana coal mine's EIS, was held premature in August 2024 because the deadline had not yet passed, and was later dismissed by agreement (Signal Peak v. Haaland); the final EIS and decision came in June 2025, about six months after the deadline the company claimed (90 FR). The Institute for Progress finds that the general remedy for agency delay, an unreasonable-delay claim under the APA, almost never succeeds. Most EISs still miss the 2-year mark: BPC found 39% of EISs finished after the 2023 law met it (BPC), and agencies reported only 2 EISs and 153 assessments as missing their deadlines in the law's first two years, a count that treats an extended deadline as met (CEQ). The bill requires the sponsor's approval for any extension and gives courts 120 days to rule.
  • Since July 2025, a sponsor can pay 125% of the agency's costs to have an EIS finished within 1 year of the notice of intent, or an assessment within 180 days (42 U.S.C. 4336f). The charts do not assume it.
  • CEQ's NEPA regulations were withdrawn in 2025, and agencies now follow their own procedures. Interior adopted interim procedures in July 2025 and final ones effective February 24, 2026 (91 FR 8738).
  • Since April 28, 2026, BLM has excluded geothermal exploration operations from detailed NEPA review, and since January 2025 resource-confirmation drilling of up to 20 acres (91 FR, Apr. 28, 2026). The geothermal "Deadlines met" case assumes both.
  • In Seven County v. Eagle County (2025), the Supreme Court told courts to defer to agencies on NEPA and said a flawed EIS does not necessarily require vacating the approval.
  • Projects covered by FAST-41 already face a 2-year limit on lawsuits (42 U.S.C. 4370m-6). Coverage is narrow: 46 projects had active timetables in March 2026, and 23 of those had been extended, 14 by more than a year (Permitting Council). On the Permitting Dashboard, 20 of 40 FAST-41 wind, solar, transmission and gas pipeline projects that are complete or under review finished, or are now due to finish, more than a month after their original timetable, including 8 of 10 offshore wind projects and all 6 solar projects but none of 9 gas pipelines. Across all 60 such projects, including cancelled and paused ones, agencies gave a reason for a delay on 15: 13 cited internal agency or interagency factors and 3 the sponsor (one cited both). Reasons are recorded for fewer than 1 in 5 delayed milestones (dashboard data compiled for this page).
  • Interior's July 2025 memo requiring Secretary-level review of wind and solar actions, and the capacity-density test in Secretarial Order 3438, were preliminarily enjoined in April 2026 only for members of the plaintiff trade groups (D. Mass. order). Interior has appealed to the First Circuit (CRS). A court vacated the January 2025 pause on wind permits in December 2025, and the government dropped its appeal (Harvard EELP).
  • Wind and solar projects that start construction after July 4, 2026 must be in service by the end of 2027 to claim the main federal tax credits, so none of the wind or solar timelines shown would qualify (IRS Notice 2025-42).
