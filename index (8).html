How to read the charts

  • Range chart. Open circles assume agencies already meet today's deadlines; filled circles assume the delays and suits of recent cases. The line between them is the range for each model project.
  • Real projects. Each dot applies the bill's limits to a project's recorded dates: the EIS capped at 28 months from the NOI (the 2-year deadline plus a 4-month sponsor suit), the decision at 2 months after the final EIS, and BLM's recorded wait before review at 30 days to answer an application and 4 months to the NOI. Other time is left as recorded, so dots run low where agencies held files without a record and high where sponsors changed projects mid-review. Hollow dots are still under review and use the agency's current schedule. Geothermal has too few recorded reviews to replay.
  • Timeline. "Show" picks the regime drawn in full; the other regime appears as a thin outline in the same row. Striped bars are delays that could happen but are not counted. Diamonds mark the federal decision and the in-service date.
  • Scenarios. In "Deadlines met" the main review runs the same length under both regimes, isolating the bill's other changes. In "Delays and challenges" reviews run as long as recent cases did; under the bill they run past the deadline until the sponsor's suit forces a decision. Developer work is held equal.
  • Time savings by provision. Provisions overlap, so each bar is the provision's average contribution across every order in which they could take effect (a Shapley value), and the bars add up to the total. Tooltips give the months each provision saves alone and added last.
  • Not modeled. The odds of a suit: suits followed 28% of large energy and transport EISs finished from 2010 to 2018, about two thirds of solar, half of pipelines, 38% of wind and 31% of transmission (Bennon and Wilson). Supporters such as FAI's Thomas Hochman expect the limits on court remedies to shorten reviews; the Institute for Progress warns shorter reviews may draw more suits. Interconnection, power contracts and tax credits are also not modeled.

What current law already does

  • Since 2023, NEPA has required an environmental assessment within 1 year and an EIS within 2 years, counted from the earliest of the agency's decision on the level of review, its notice that a right-of-way application is complete, or the notice of intent. Agencies can extend these deadlines after consulting the applicant, and sponsors can already ask a court to enforce them (42 U.S.C. 4336a). A court can set a new deadline of up to 90 days, but the one sponsor suit under this provision we found, over a Montana coal mine's EIS, was dismissed in August 2024 as premature because the deadline had not yet passed (Signal Peak v. Haaland); the final EIS and decision came in June 2025, about six months after the deadline the company claimed (90 FR). The Institute for Progress finds that the general remedy for agency delay, an unreasonable-delay claim under the APA, almost never succeeds. Most EISs still miss the 2-year mark: BPC found 39% of EISs finished after the 2023 law met it (BPC), and agencies reported only 2 EISs and 153 assessments as missing their deadlines in the law's first two years, a count that treats an extended deadline as met (CEQ). The bill requires the sponsor's approval for any extension and gives courts 120 days to rule.
  • Since July 2025, a sponsor can pay 125% of the agency's costs to have an EIS finished within 1 year of the notice of intent, or an assessment within 180 days (42 U.S.C. 4336f). The charts do not assume it.
  • CEQ's NEPA regulations were withdrawn in 2025, and agencies now follow their own procedures. Interior adopted interim procedures in July 2025 and final ones effective February 24, 2026 (91 FR 8738).
  • In Seven County v. Eagle County (2025), the Supreme Court told courts to defer to agencies on NEPA and said a flawed EIS does not necessarily require vacating the approval.
  • Projects covered by FAST-41 already face a 2-year limit on lawsuits (42 U.S.C. 4370m-6). Coverage is narrow: 46 projects had active timetables in March 2026, and 23 of those had been extended, 14 by more than a year (Permitting Council). On the Permitting Dashboard, 20 of 40 FAST-41 wind, solar, transmission and gas pipeline projects that are complete or under review finished, or are now due to finish, more than a month after their original timetable, including 8 of 10 offshore wind projects and all 6 solar projects but none of 9 gas pipelines. Of 15 projects whose agencies gave a reason for a delay, 13 cited internal agency or interagency factors and 3 cited the sponsor (one cited both); reasons are recorded for fewer than 1 in 5 delayed milestones (dashboard data compiled for this page).
  • Interior's July 2025 memo requiring Secretary-level review of wind and solar actions, and the capacity-density test in Secretarial Order 3438, were preliminarily enjoined in April 2026 only for members of the plaintiff trade groups (Crowell). Interior has appealed to the First Circuit (CRS). A court vacated the January 2025 pause on wind permits in December 2025, and the government dropped its appeal (Harvard EELP).
  • Wind and solar projects that start construction after July 4, 2026 must be in service by the end of 2027 to claim the main federal tax credits, so none of the wind or solar timelines shown would qualify (IRS Notice 2025-42).
